The ruleread

One item per building, on one machine type. A constructor floor and an assembler floor do not want the same lane widths, lift positions or door spacing, and refineries want none of it — so they get separate shells, which also makes each shell blueprintable.

Commodities are made at their source and arrive on a bus: iron, copper and steel ingot, plastic, rubber, concrete, silica. Fluids never leave their site. The bus runs in the road corridors, and the Depot yard taps it rather than running its own belts across town.

Every site gets its own terminal, container row and sink, sized to what that site makes — the Depot is global central storage, so an item needs exactly one depot anywhere in the world, at the place it is made. The town stores its eight, concrete gets its depot at the limestone node, steel and its parts at Site C, polymers at Site D.

Storage is one place per site, not many: the depot row. Buildings hold nothing — no input buffers, no output containers — they just belt their item onto the bus, and every lane ends at the row. There each item gets a container with a Depot on top and a Smart Splitter on its feed, overflow lifting onto a shared sushi belt that ends in a hand-feedable container in front of the sink.

No "pull from next door" exception any more. Reinforced plate comes from the reinforced plate building whether that is one plot away or four.

Belt bandwidth decides the column order. One item per building means that building’s whole output has to leave on belts, and a lane caps at the current mark. Anything over one lane goes point to point to its main consumer — the screw building sits directly above the rotor building for exactly this reason — and only the remainder joins the bus.

Every size is foundations first. A plan of 8 × 6 means eight foundations east by six south, which is 64 × 48 m. The metre figure is there to sanity-check, never to build to.

One, two or three floors. Nothing stacks higher, and no plant is a tower.

These positions are a sketch, not a survey. The ground will have opinions — a rock where a constructor goes, a slope across half a plot, a lake edge that is not where you want it. Move the plot. Each building carries a siting note saying what it is actually pinned to, and most are pinned to nothing.

Phases 2 to 5 still show the older grouping — plants that make their own intermediates. They need the same treatment and it is the next job; the counts there will drop the same way Phase 1’s did.

Set target output, not clock percent. Every clocked line below gives a per-machine output figure — type that into the machine and it derives the exact clock, so awkward numbers like 133.3% cost you nothing. The rate on each line is the whole building; the figure after the recipe is what one machine gets set to.

Overclock to save a machine where the node is the cap. Power scales at about clock^1.3, so pushing a constructor to 20 concrete costs roughly 10% more power per unit while removing a third of the machines and a belt leg — worth it when a node ceiling rather than the grid is what limits the line. The power panel uses nominal draw, so underclocked buildings come in under what it shows.

Machines in a single row along the 48 m axis, not opposed banks. A constructor is about 8 × 10 m, so three across a 4-foundation shed leaves 4 m at each side — not enough for a belt lane and somewhere to stand. Five along the long wall is 40 m with 4 m spare at each end: pull them 3 m off the wall for the output merger, manifold in front, and the eastern half of the floor stays clear. Nothing in the town needs more than five, so opposed banks never apply here.

Blueprint designers are 4 × 4, 5 × 5 and 6 × 6, so a whole 4 × 6 shed only becomes stampable at Mk.3. Since each shed is built once and is not meant to expand, that is not worth designing around — use whatever arrangement suits the machine count and let the sheds differ inside.

Reserve two foundations more than the plan in each direction. Terrain eats the difference, and it leaves room for belts round the outside rather than through the middle. Lifting a plant onto stilts is usually cheaper than terracing the hill it sits on.

Grid anchor

Each phase has its own campus and its own anchor, in metres — stand on the spot, read your position, type it in. Every building then gets a coordinate to walk to, rounded to the nearest 10 m: near enough to find, not precise enough to build to.

Site A is set to your HUB at roughly X −560, Y 2369. Confirm the X, I only had two digits of it.

Phase 2 sits on its own ground, so the two campuses never need a belt between them. Everything you hand-build at Site B or C comes out of the Dimensional Depot instead.

The Depot feeds your inventory, not machines. It solves construction at a distance, not production — every plant at Site C still needs ore and ingots from nodes at Site C.
